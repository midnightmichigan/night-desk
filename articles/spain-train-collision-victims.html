Spain train collision — 4 Americans among 69 dead, all but three victims now identified

Four U.S. citizens were among the 69 people killed in last week's high-speed train collision in central Spain, authorities confirmed — one of Europe's deadliest rail disasters this century.

The Center for Integrated Data, the legal body in charge of identifying victims in mass-fatality events, said late Sunday that the dead also included one citizen each from Canada, Japan, Argentina, Mexico, Tunisia and the Czech Republic, alongside a French citizen, a Portuguese citizen, and 54 Spaniards announced earlier.

All but three of the victims have now been identified. Three missing-person reports remain active, concerning two Australians and one Japanese citizen, authorities said.

What happened

The crash occurred on the morning of Oct. 28 near Urda, in the province of Toledo, about 130 kilometres south of Madrid. A high-speed train operated by state operator Renfe slammed into the rear of a stationary train operated by private company Iryo. Two carriages derailed and 58 people were injured.

Both trains were heading in the same direction, from Madrid to Seville, with more than 540 people on board. Independent investigators said their initial findings indicated the Renfe train was travelling at between 100 and 125 mph when it struck the Iryo train.

A failure of an automatic safety system had halted all trains on a 63-kilometre stretch of track, but a control centre authorised the Renfe service to resume its journey while the other train was stationary. Transport Minister Óscar Puente has attributed much of what went wrong to poor judgement by a traffic controller.

The identification wait

Forensics teams have identified victims through fingerprints and DNA — in some cases, authorities said, from individual body parts. Families of some victims have said they want to travel to Spain to complete the identification process in person, and officials said they are awaiting those families' arrival.

This was Spain's second high-speed train collision this year, after a crash in Andalusia in January killed 46 people — a fact already raising fresh questions about rail safety on the Madrid–Seville line.

The Night Desk will update this article as Spanish authorities identify the final victims and investigators release more on how the Renfe train was cleared to move.

Facts in this article are drawn from the Associated Press report updated Nov. 2, 2026, on the identification announcement by Spain's Center for Integrated Data, and corroborating reports of the same announcement. Victim names had not been released in that report.
