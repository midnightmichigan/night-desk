Water Softener Salt, Explained: Types, How Much Your Tank Takes, and Storing It

A water softener does its work quietly until the day it doesn't — spots back on the dishes, soap that won't lather, that slippery-clean feeling gone from the shower. Nine times out of ten the fix is embarrassingly simple: the brine tank ran out of salt, possibly weeks ago. This is the short guide to what's in the bags, how much a typical system actually uses, and how to keep ahead of it.

The three kinds of salt (and the one alternative)

  • Evaporated salt pellets are the purest common option — they dissolve cleanly and leave the least residue and buildup in the brine tank. The right default for most systems, and the one manufacturers most often recommend.
  • Solar salt (crystals or pellets, made by evaporating seawater or brine in ponds) is nearly as pure, usually a little cheaper, and fine for the great majority of softeners.
  • Rock salt is the budget option and the dirtiest: it carries more insoluble material, which settles in the tank as sludge and means more frequent tank cleanouts. It saves a little per bag and charges it back in maintenance.
  • Potassium chloride is the salt-free-tasting alternative — technically not a salt at all in the sodium sense. It works in most softeners (check your manual), costs noticeably more per bag, and is the choice for households watching sodium in their water or discharge. Note it can need a small settings adjustment on some systems, since it regenerates slightly less efficiently.

How much a typical tank takes

  • Salt comes in 40 lb bags as the standard unit (some brands also sell 25 lb and 50 lb).
  • Most residential brine tanks can hold 200–300 lb of salt — but you shouldn't fill them to the top. The working rule is to keep the tank roughly one-third to one-half full: salt sitting too deep and too long compacts, and a completely full tank hides the water level you need to be able to see.
  • Top up a few bags at a time. For many households that's two to three 40 lb bags every couple of months, but usage varies a lot with water hardness, household size, and how often your system regenerates — a demand-initiated system sips; an older timer-based system regenerates on schedule whether it needs to or not.
  • The level that matters: keep dry salt a few inches above the water line in the brine well. If you can see mostly water, you're already running on empty.

The two-minute monthly check

Once a month, lift the lid and look for the two classic problems:

  • Salt bridging: a hard crust forms over hollow space — the tank looks full, but no salt is reaching the water. Poke the crust gently with a broom handle; if it collapses into a gap, that was your problem.
  • Mushing: a sludgy layer at the bottom that blocks dissolving. More common with rock salt and overfilled tanks; the fix is a manual cleanout, which is a bucket-and-afternoon job, not a service call.

A resin cleaner, run through the system periodically per its label, helps clear iron and buildup from the softener bed itself — cheap insurance, especially on well water with iron in it.

Storing the bags

Salt is heavy, thirsty, and unforgiving of damp floors. Store bags off bare concrete if you can (a pallet, shelf, or even a sheet of plywood), somewhere dry, and close to the softener — a 40 lb bag carried across a basement is a chore you'll postpone, and postponed salt is how tanks run dry. A lidded bin or tote next to the tank, kept topped up from the stored bags, makes the monthly check a one-minute job.

The short version

Evaporated pellets if you want set-and-forget, solar salt to save a little, rock salt only if you accept the cleanouts, potassium chloride if sodium is the concern. Keep the tank a third to half full, glance at it monthly, and store the spare bags dry and close. A softener almost never breaks — it just quietly runs out of the one thing it eats.
